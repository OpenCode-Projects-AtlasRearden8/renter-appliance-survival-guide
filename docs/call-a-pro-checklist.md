# The Call-a-Pro Checklist

Calling a technician with a useless story gets you a vague diagnosis and a vague bill. Calling one with facts gets you a fast diagnosis (and respect). Here's the checklist to use *before* the call — plus honest expectations after.

## Before you call: the 10-minute prep

### 1. Confirm it's genuinely pro territory

Do-it-yourself is great for lint, filters, gaskets, coils, resets, and cheap swap-in parts. Call a human pro immediately (no experiments) for:

- **STOP signals:** gas smell (call the **gas utility emergency line first**, from outside), burning/plastic smell, smoke, sparks, breaker that trips again right after one reset.
- **Water + electricity coincidence:** leak near outlets, water inside the machine's control panel area, breaker first then tech.
- **Roof, refrigerant, heat exchanger, gas valves:** legally/practically pro-only. Don't watch three videos and reconsider — these are the ones that hurt people.

### 2. Gather the facts (the sheet earns its keep)

- [ ] Model number (sticker: door frame, back, or toe-kick) — not the serial
- [ ] [Error-code log sheet](error-code-log-sheet.html) entries with dates
- [ ] What you already tried (cleaned X, reset Y, part Z replaced) — this is the part techs respect most, and it already rules things out
- [ ] When it started, and whether it's constant or intermittent
- [ ] Sounds it makes (thump, grind, click, hum-then-silence) — recordings help
- [ ] Lease/landlord status if renting — appliance failures usually belong to the landlord; you want this known *before* labor starts

### 3. Know the honesty of what you're asking for

A good call sounds like: *"Frigidaire washer, model XXX, drain-fault code three times in two weeks — cleaned the pump filter each time, power-cycled, hose is clear. It drains on cancel cycles. Suspect the pressure switch or pump, but I'd rather you diagnose it."* That call gets a tech arriving with the right parts. A bad call — *"it's been weird for a while"* — gets a diagnostic hour you pay for while they find the basics.

## Asking smart, non-condescending questions (they don't bite)

- "What's your diagnostic fee, and does it roll into the repair if I go ahead?" *(Industry standard: yes it usually rolls in. Clarify before booking.)*
- "Rough range for [part name] plus labor on this model?" *(Any tech giving straight answers has a range. Vague forever = run.)*
- "Which items on your estimate are must-fix-now vs. can-wait?" *(Filters, hoses, belts: often legitimately replaceable at visit time; compressors: decisions.)*
- "Will the fix use OEM or equivalent parts, and what warranty comes with it?" *(90 days–1 year on the repair is common.)*
- *(If renting)*: "May I get the diagnosis in writing so I can loop in my landlord?"

## After the visit: what reasonable looks like

- An **itemized estimate** (parts/labor separated), before work proceeds.
- The tech **shows or explains** what failed — you get to see the sad part.
- A written **warranty** on the repair, honored without attitude if it fails early.
- Cleaner appliance areas than they found — techs wash their hands; so do shoes; good ones bring their own mats.

**Red flags:** cash-only no-receipt, a vague "you need a whole new [X]" attached to a visit nobody scheduled for that, refusal to say a price range, no warranty. One "whole new machine" opinion with no diagnosis deserves a second opinion — it's a phone call to another company, not a divorce.

## If you rent — the sequence that works

1. Do your [FREE maintenance yourself](how-to-use.html) (this is already your baseline behavior).
2. Report failures **in writing** (even text works), with: symptom, first notice date, what you tried, model number. 
3. Follow up once in writing after 3 business days if radio silence, then once more politely.
4. Ask what vendor they use, and greet that vendor with the same fact-checklist above — most landlords are choosing someone to *trust*, and a tenant whose report is precise makes that easy.
5. **Safety items** (leaks onto power, gas smell, no heat in winter): escalate to a phone call immediately — this is not an email-patience category, and documentation can happen *after*.
