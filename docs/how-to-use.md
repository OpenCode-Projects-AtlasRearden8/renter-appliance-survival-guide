# How to Use This Guide (Really, Read This One First)

This is not a repair manual. It's a *decoding* manual. Here's how to get the most out of it.

## The three questions this guide answers

For every appliance — dishwasher, washer, dryer, fridge, HVAC — each chapter covers:

1. **What error codes honestly mean.** Spoiler: a code usually means "a sensor noticed something," not "this part is dead." We'll tell you which codes are worth chasing and which ones just mean "clean something."
2. **What's worth cleaning monthly.** There are exactly a handful of cheap, boring maintenance tasks that prevent most emergency calls. Filters, coils, gaskets, vents. We'll show you where they hide.
3. **When to call a pro.** Some jobs you do with a sponge. Some jobs you do with a phone. The line between them matters — and we draw it clearly, because electricity, gas, and water are not DIY territory for beginners.

## Three habits that make this guide work

### 1. Write the code down *first*

When an appliance flashes `E24` or `F5 E3`, the single most useful thing you can do is write it down — appliance, date, what you were doing, the exact code. Use the [error-code log sheet](error-code-log-sheet.html). Why? Because:

- Some codes are stored and only show the *oldest* one; knowing the history helps.
- If you're about to search the web, a written-down code beats a half-remembered one.
- If you do need a technician, a log turns "it's been acting weird" into useful facts.

### 2. Reset before you panic

A huge number of appliance calls are fixed by cutting power for a minute. Every chapter has a reset section, and there's a general [reset basics](reset-basics.html) chapter. It's free, it takes a minute, and it's safe.

### 3. Know your appliance's "cheap parts" list

Each appliance has 3–5 parts that are cheap, user-replaceable, and cause most of the codes: filters, door gaskets, lint traps, water inlet filters. Replace those before you replace the appliance.

## Symbols we use

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **STOP** | Stop right there. This is pro (or utility company) territory: gas smell, burning smell, standing water near power, breaker issues, anything on the roof. |
| **FREE** | Costs nothing but a few minutes: resets, cleaning, checking basics. |
| **CHEAP** | Under ~$25 and beginner-replaceable: a filter, gasket, or fuse. |
| **PRO** | Call a technician. Not because you're incapable — because the risk isn't worth it. |

## The renter twist: you might not own it

If you rent, some of this isn't yours to fix — it's your landlord's. Quick rules of thumb (check your lease; it wins):

- **Usually your job:** cleaning filters, gaskets, coils, lint traps — everything in the *FREE* category.
- **Usually the landlord's job:** the appliance itself breaking, plumbing, wiring, HVAC refrigerant — anything involving tools or parts.
- **Pro move:** do the *FREE* stuff yourself, then report bigger problems in writing (even just a text: "Fridge is not cooling, can you send someone?") so there's a record and a date.

## Safety, the short version, every time

- **Unplug it** (or flip the breaker) before sticking hands where they don't belong.
- **Water and electricity:** if an appliance is leaking onto anything electrical, cut power first, clean second.
- **Gas smell:** leave the house, call the gas utility's emergency line. Not a big-decision moment — just go.
- **Fire smell or smoke** from dryer, dishwasher, or anything: stop using it and read the [call-a-pro checklist](call-a-pro-checklist.html).
